Teams usually have plenty of documents and no reliable way to turn them into knowledge they can find, check, and update.

I use LLM Wiki for that. It is an LLM-compiled knowledge base built from Markdown files. Source material stays as evidence. The LLM reads the sources and writes smaller, connected articles with metadata, links, and references back to the original material. Codex can then query the wiki during an engineering task.

RAG is the obvious comparison, but the working model differs. A typical RAG system retrieves chunks when a question arrives and generates an answer for that request. LLM Wiki also writes durable articles before the next question exists. I can open those articles in Obsidian, edit them, check their sources, and reuse them without repeating the whole synthesis step.

## The model I use

My wiki has three layers:

```text
source documents
    -> raw files with provenance
    -> compiled Markdown articles
    -> indexes used for navigation and queries
```

The raw layer is the evidence. Normal compilation does not rewrite it. The compiled layer contains readable articles organized by topic, concept, or reference. Indexes are derived data, so they can be rebuilt from the files.

Separating the layers makes errors findable. If an answer looks wrong, I trace it to an article and then back to the source. If an index breaks, the Markdown is still there. Search helps me find things; it does not hold the only copy.

## Why I separate topics

I do not put every document into one large knowledge base. Each subject gets its own topic wiki with separate sources, articles, indexes, and activity logs.

Retrieval stays focused. An onboarding question does not pull context from an unrelated product analysis. Each topic also has its own lifecycle: I can compile one, leave another in the intake queue, or archive it without touching the rest of the hub.

The files are still plain Markdown with YAML frontmatter. A compiled article records title, summary, tags, source paths, update date, and confidence. Those fields make the collection easier to inspect without opening every document.

## How I use it

I use two topic wikis right now.

The first supports engineering onboarding. I ingested five DingTalk knowledge nodes covering onboarding, system architecture, navigation, shared utilities, and local port conflicts. Those sources sit in the raw layer and have not been compiled. I left them there on purpose. Collecting a document and turning it into maintained guidance are separate decisions.

The second topic supports product and engineering research. I ingested two PDFs of 13 and 29 pages. LLM Wiki compiled those 42 pages into four linked articles on product scope, technical routing and evidence, measurement questions, and reference analysis.

The useful result was not a shorter PDF. The wiki separated confirmed source material from engineering suggestions and open decisions. One article describes the product boundary. Another keeps unresolved metrics and permission questions visible. Links connect the two without pushing everything into one long summary.

My normal workflow:

```text
1. Create a focused topic wiki
2. Ingest documents without changing the originals
3. Check source metadata and provenance
4. Compile new sources into connected articles
5. Review claims, confidence, and unresolved gaps
6. Lint the wiki and query it during later work
```

Compilation is incremental by default. When I add a source, the system processes the new material instead of rebuilding every article. Each write also lands in an append-only activity log, so I can reconstruct what happened to a topic later.

## What the LLM is allowed to do

The LLM is a compiler here, not the source of truth. Its job is to organize evidence, combine related material, and write useful connections. It should not fill missing facts with plausible text.

I expect generated articles to keep source paths and state uncertainty directly. In the product research topic, suggested architecture and target metrics remain labelled as proposals, not implemented behavior. Questions that still need an owner or a decision stay open instead of being rewritten as conclusions.

A fluent article can still be wrong, so generated content gets a closer review. I check whether a claim has a source, whether a recommendation is presented as a fact, and whether disagreement between documents survived the edit.

## Where it helps

LLM Wiki works well for material that will be read more than once: engineering onboarding, architecture notes, product research, incident knowledge, and a growing set of technical references.

A one-off question with no long-term value does not justify the setup. Creating a topic, maintaining sources, and compiling articles would add work without improving the next task. I also would not use it as a replacement for an operational database, an access-control system, or the original source archive.

## What this changes in my workflow

I used to treat document search and document writing as separate tasks. Now they share one pipeline. The same source that answers a question can become part of a maintained article, and the article keeps enough provenance for the next engineer to inspect it.

The boundary between immutable sources, generated articles, and rebuildable indexes is the interesting part. In practice, I can take a mixed set of project documents, turn them into a maintainable knowledge system, and keep the output tied to evidence.

I still make the final editorial decisions. The LLM reduces the cost of reading, grouping, and drafting. It does not decide which internal information is safe to publish, whether a recommendation is approved, or when an article is accurate enough to guide engineering work.

## Where this stands

I do not think of LLM Wiki as chatting with a folder. It is a compilation step for knowledge that has to outlive one conversation. Markdown keeps the output inspectable, and provenance lets me account for any claim. Topic boundaries keep retrieval from mixing unrelated material.

The two wikis are still small, which helps at this stage. I can see where the workflow saves time and where human review is still required before I trust it with a larger engineering knowledge base.

大多数团队并不缺文档，缺的是一种可靠的方法，把文档整理成能查找、能核对、也能持续更新的知识。

我用 LLM Wiki 补的就是这个缺口。它是一个由 LLM 编译的 Markdown 知识库。原始资料作为证据保留下来，LLM 读完这些资料，再写出更聚焦、互相关联的文章。文章带元数据、链接和指向原文的引用。以后做工程任务时，Codex 可以直接查询这些内容。

它乍看和 RAG 很像，工作方式其实不同。常见的 RAG 系统在收到问题后检索文本片段，再为当次请求生成答案。LLM Wiki 会在下一个问题出现之前就把文章写出来。我可以在 Obsidian 里打开、修改、核对来源，也可以重复使用这些文章，不必每次重新做一遍整理。

## 我使用的知识模型

我的 Wiki 分成三层：

```text
原始文档
    -> 带来源信息的 raw 文件
    -> 编译后的 Markdown 文章
    -> 用于导航和查询的索引
```

raw 层保存证据，正常的编译过程不会改写它。编译层保存可读的文章，可以按主题、概念或参考资料组织。索引属于派生数据，损坏了也能从文件重新生成。

层与层分开，出错时才能定位。如果答案有问题，我先找到对应文章，再回到原始资料核对。如果索引坏了，Markdown 文件还在。搜索层提高查找效率，但不是知识的唯一副本。

## 为什么要按主题隔离

我不会把所有文档塞进一个大型知识库。每个领域有自己的 topic wiki，来源、文章、索引和操作日志都分开管理。

检索范围也更容易控制。入职问题不该混进另一项产品分析的上下文。每个主题也能有自己的节奏：我可以编译一个，把另一个留在资料队列里，或者单独归档，不影响整个 hub。

底层文件仍然是普通的 Markdown 加 YAML frontmatter。编译后的文章记录标题、摘要、标签、来源路径、更新时间和置信度。看这些字段就能先判断相关性，不用逐个打开文件。

## 我在实际工作中怎样使用

我目前维护两个 topic wiki。

第一个用于工程研发入门。我从钉钉导入了 5 个知识节点，内容涉及入职、系统架构、网址导航、公共工具和本地端口冲突。这些资料还留在 raw 层，没有进入编译。我是故意不编译的：收集资料和整理成可维护的说明，是两个决定。资料可以先放在队列里，等资料齐全、边界清楚了再动。

第二个主题用于产品与工程研究。我导入了两份 PDF，分别是 13 页和 29 页。LLM Wiki 把这 42 页资料整理成 4 篇互相关联的文章，分别讨论产品范围、技术路由与证据、指标问题和参考分析。

有用的结果并不是一份更短的 PDF。Wiki 把原文已确认的内容、工程建议和待决策问题分开了。一篇文章说明产品边界，另一篇保留尚未确定的指标与权限问题，链接把两者连起来，不必把所有信息挤进一篇长总结。

我的常用流程是：

```text
1. 创建边界明确的 topic wiki
2. 导入资料，同时保留原文
3. 检查来源信息和元数据
4. 把新增资料编译成互相关联的文章
5. 审查结论、置信度和未解决问题
6. 运行 lint，并在后续任务中查询 Wiki
```

默认只编译增量部分。新增资料时，系统只处理新内容，不必重建全部文章。每次写入还会追加到操作日志，之后回看时，能知道一个主题经历了哪些变化。

## LLM 可以做什么

在这个系统里，LLM 是编译器，不是事实来源。它负责组织证据、合并相关资料、建立文章之间的联系，但不能用听起来合理的文字补齐缺失事实。

我要求生成的文章保留来源路径，并直接写明不确定性。在产品研究主题中，建议采用的架构和目标指标仍被标为方案，而不是已经上线的能力。需要负责人确认的问题留在待决策清单里，不会被改写成确定结论。

文章读起来顺畅，却仍然可能出错，所以生成的内容要按另一套标准审查。我会看结论有没有来源，建议有没有被写成事实，不同文档之间的分歧有没有保留下来。

## 它适合哪些工作

LLM Wiki 适合会被反复使用的资料：工程入门、架构笔记、产品研究、事故经验，以及数量不断增加的技术参考。

如果只是一次性问题，内容没有长期价值，专门建一套 Wiki 就不划算。创建主题、维护来源、编译文章都要花时间，对下一个任务没有帮助。它也不能替代业务数据库、权限系统或原始资料存档。

## 它怎样改变我的工作流

以前我把查文档和写文档当成两件独立的事。现在它们共用一条流程。回答过问题的资料可以继续整理成长期维护的文章，文章保留来源信息，下一位工程师可以接着核对。

我自己最在意的是这三者之间的边界：不能随手改的原始资料、由 LLM 生成的文章，以及可以重新构建的索引。落到实际工作上，我可以把一堆混杂的项目文档整理成可维护的知识系统，同时让生成内容始终能回到证据。

最终的编辑决定仍然由我负责。LLM 减少了阅读、归类和起草的工作量，但它不能替我判断哪些内部信息可以公开，某项建议是否已经通过，或者一篇文章是否准确到可以指导工程实施。

## 目前的判断

我不把 LLM Wiki 当成“和一个文件夹聊天”。它是一套编译流程，处理的是需要跨越单次对话的知识。Markdown 让内容可以检查，来源记录让结论可以被追问，主题隔离则避免检索时混入无关资料。

两个 Wiki 现在规模都不大，在这个阶段反而是好事。我能看清哪些步骤省了时间、哪些地方还得人工审核，然后再决定要不要把它用在更大的工程知识库上。
